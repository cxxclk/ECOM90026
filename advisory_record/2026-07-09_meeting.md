## Part 1: Main line and model specification

### 1.1 Research question and one-sentence answer

**Research question**: The 2016 Housing Purchase Restrictions (HPR) pushed housing demand into neighbouring unrestricted cities and drove up local house prices — under this price shock, which families stopped having children, and where did the money go?

**One-sentence answer**: The price shock did not lower the average fertility rate — it precisely suppressed **the "trying for a son" second-birth margin of one-child families whose firstborn is a daughter**, and these families redirected resources into the education of the existing daughter (quantity→quality substitution).

**Identification strategy**: the HPR spillover design of Liu & Zhang (2025) — treated = unrestricted prefecture-level city within ≤250 km of the nearest restricted city; all 22 restricted source cities are dropped. The spillover of demand from restricted cities is a quasi-exogenous shock to house prices in neighbouring cities.

**Differentiation from L&Z**: their margin is marriage-market entry + education costs for rural people without urban property, and the outcome is the number of births among all women of childbearing age; here the margin is **within-marriage parity progression × son preference × Q→Q substitution** — the mechanisms and outcome sets do not overlap. Their Table 3 has been replicated (1.6a), and the identification is valid; their significance is conditional on linear pre-trend adjustment, while the results here do not depend on trend adjustment.

**Treatment of the universal two-child policy**: the universal two-child policy (2016.1), almost simultaneous with HPR, is not treated as a mere confounder but as an object of study — **housing costs determined who responded to the two-child policy**. The daughter group first shows a policy-response boom in 2016–17 and is then crushed by prices; the son group (the margin newly opened by the policy) is inelastic to price throughout.

### 1.2 Data and sample construction

- CFPS 2010–2022, seven waves. Birth outcomes do not use wave-level self-reports; instead, **annual birth sequences are reconstructed** from the famconf child birth-year roster (using the latest roster; the contemporaneous roster systematically underreports by 20–35%, which is the main reason for abandoning the wave-level design, 1.4e).
- Geography: the `顺序码匹配.dta` crosswalk (countyid → prefecture-level city, 99.9% validated, covering ~85% of the analysis sample).
- Treatment variable: `treat_hpr` assigned by **baseline** city (to prevent migration self-selection); the HPR city list is taken from L&Z Tables A.1/A.2; distances use shapefile city centroids; centroid vs boundary and 200/250/300 km cutoffs have all been verified as robust (`diagnose_distance.py`).
- Main sample (annual hazard): married women 20–45, unrestricted prefectures, 2009–2022, ~113 prefecture clusters. One-child risk set 38,614 woman-years (daughter arm 17,000 / 3,176 women, son arm 23,108 / 3,816).

### 1.3 Four-layer empirical arc

#### Layer 1 — Average effect ≈ 0

| Specification | Coefficient | p | N |
|---|---|---|---|
| Any-birth pooled hazard, Post=2018 | −0.63pp (baseline 7.4%) | 0.128 | 88,942 |
| Same, Post=2016 | +0.08pp | 0.871 | 88,942 |

The first-birth margin fails the pre-trend test (2011/2013 significantly positive; the childless risk set is contaminated by late-marriage selection), so we report only "no suppression of the entry-into-motherhood margin was found", not a clean estimate. The average does not move; all the action is in parity progression — 1.5 gives two structural explanations of ≈0.

#### Layer 2 — The margin is the second birth (by parity)

| Transition (risk set) | DID (Post=2018) | pre-trends | N (woman-years) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Second birth (one-child families) | **−1.2pp / baseline 7.0% (p=0.104)** | 2010–14 all flat | 38,614 |
| Third+ birth (2+ families) | **−0.05pp (p=0.87)** = clean placebo | flat | 36,236 |

- The pooled layer-2 number is the value re-run after the 6/25 audit (p≈0.10; the old recorded p=0.052 is void). The pooled p=0.10 is consistent with the design: 58% of the one-child risk set are son families (which do not respond), so the pooled coefficient is diluted (−2.38pp × 42% ≈ −1.0pp, matching the observed value) — the gender split (layer 3) is the design itself.
- Third+ births exactly zero: 2+ families have no son-seeking margin, a placebo as predicted.

#### Layer 3 — The margin is gendered (headline)

**Split-sample DID (Post=2018, annual hazard)**:

| Arm | T×post | SE | p | Baseline hazard | N |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| Firstborn son (placebo) | −0.57pp | 0.0076 | 0.456 | 5.7% | 23,108 |

**DDD (single equation, N=40,108)**: T×post **−2.44pp (p=0.019)**; T×post×son **+1.82pp (p=0.091)**; T×son **−2.14pp (p=0.006)** — in the pre-period the daughter group has a real level premium in treated areas, and this term must be controlled (1.4c).

**Split-window DID (run additionally on 2026-07-08, `split_post_did.py`)** — single-year coefficients (SE ~2pp) have no power; split windows are the right granularity for the test:

| Window | Daughter arm | Son arm (placebo) |
|---|---|---|
| boom 2016–17 | +2.45pp (p=0.075) | +0.18pp (p=0.88) |
| post 2018–19 | **−0.38pp (p=0.78) ≈ 0** | −0.00pp (p=1.00) |
| post 2020–22 | **−3.22pp (p=0.013)**; with the boom put into the baseline **−3.75pp (p=0.003)** | −1.02pp (p=0.29) |

At the raw level, the second-birth premium of the treated daughter group: pre +3.9pp → boom **+6.0pp** → 2018–19 +3.1pp (back to the pre level) → 2020–22 **+0.5pp (erased)**.

**How to read the timeline**: the power of the single Post=2018 coefficient (−2.38pp) comes from 2020–22. This has economic content and is not an anomaly — (i) the two-child policy **brought forward** son-seeking demand: those most eager to have a child rushed to do so in 2016–17 and exited the risk set, and 2018–19 is the aftermath of the boom; (ii) birth lag: house-price pressure bites on pregnancy decisions from 2019 onward, which become visible in birth counts only from 2020; (iii) the event study split by norm halves (2.3d) shows that the "zero" of 2018–19 is a composite: **high-norm cities begin to decline already in 2018** (−1.9 → −3.4 → −5.1 → −7.3pp), while low-norm cities are still in the tail of the boom in the same period (2017 +4.0 / 2019 +4.6pp), and the two offset each other. Where the margin really exists, the suppression starts in 2018 and deepens year by year.
This also explains why the Post=2016 specification is ≈0 (−0.23pp, p=0.82) — it averages away the boom and the suppression. Post=2018 is an ex-ante choice (following L&Z: restrictions took effect at end-2016 + pregnancy lag), and the cut point is not changed because of the time shape seen ex post.

**Other robustness**: + group linear trends → the daughter group strengthens to −3.48pp (p=0.018), trend term ≈0 (no reliance on trend extrapolation; the L&Z average effect does rely on it); the son group stays flat with trends. Firstborn sex is balanced T/C (0.5855 vs 0.5854, p=0.86), ruling out sex-selective entry.

**Why the gender result is the contribution**: two ex-ante channels predict opposite signs — the marriage-housing obligation channel (son = future marriage-housing liability ⇒ should suppress son families) is falsified; the son-preference replacement-fertility channel (daughter families are the active "try one more" margin) wins.
**Supporting evidence — sex-selection decomposition** (`sex_selection.py`): second births in the daughter group are sex-selected at baseline (male share 0.565 vs natural ~0.512); the shock suppresses son births (−1.74pp, p=0.056) far more than daughter births (−0.64pp, p=0.30), and 73% of the total suppression comes from son births; the son group's baseline sex ratio is 0.503 (natural), with no asymmetry. ⇒ The price shock precisely suppresses sex-selective son-seeking behaviour.

#### Layer 4 — Quantity→Quality substitution (wave level; outcome measured only in survey waves)

The same daughter one-child families, education spending equation (same sample split and right-hand side as the fertility equation, only the outcome changes):

| Outcome | Coefficient | p |
|---|---|---|
| Daughter group ihs(education spending) DID | **+0.89** | **0.034** |
| Daughter group extensive margin (spending>0) | **+10.5pp** | **0.026** |
| Daughter group ES path (by wave) | +0.55 → +0.47 → +1.27 → +1.46 | —, —, 0.022, 0.027 |
| Son group | +0.56 | 0.099 |
| Gender difference | not significant | 0.28 |
| 2+ child families (no quantity margin) benchmark | −0.12 | 0.64 |

- The binding logic is parallel reduced forms, not a mediation regression (`new_birth` is a post-treatment variable; putting it on the right-hand side = bad control). The "substitution" is bound together by four pieces: in the daughter group births↓ and education↑; the ES time paths mirror each other; the son group is weak on both; 2+ families show no education response (ruling out a pure income/price effect — if families had simply become richer or education more expensive, 2+ families should also move).
- Boundary of the claim: we only say "within daughter families: fertility↓, education↑", and do not claim that the education response differs by gender (difference p=0.28).
- `edu_exp` is at the household level ⇒ clean only for one-child families (all spending = the only child), which is exactly this sample.

### 1.4 Model specification

#### a. Notation and sample
i = woman, c(i) = prefecture-level city at baseline, t = year. T_i ∈ {0,1}: baseline city is unrestricted and ≤250 km. Post_t = 1{t≥2018}. son_i: sex of the firstborn (latest famconf roster, `tb2_a_c*`, 1=male). In all specifications SE are clustered by prefecture (~113 clusters).

#### b. Main design — annual second-birth hazard DID
Risk set (discrete-time hazard): i enters the sample in year t ⟺ she has exactly one child as of the end of year t−1, i.e. t ∈ (firstby_i, min(secondby_i, lastwave_i, 2022)], and age 20–45; she exits after a second birth; women whose first birth is twins never enter.

$$y_{it} = \beta\,(T_i \times Post_t) + \gamma_1 age_{it} + \gamma_2 age_{it}^2 + \gamma_3 dur_{it} + \gamma_4 dur_{it}^2 + \mu_{c(i)} + \lambda_t + \varepsilon_{it}$$

- y_it = 1{i has a second birth in year t}; dur = t − firstby (duration dependence); μ_c city FE, λ_t year FE (the main effects of T and Post are absorbed respectively).
- No individual FE: the treatment variation is in the cross-section of prefectures, and individual FE would absorb the identifying variation; in a hazard panel, individuals entering and exiting the risk set also make the within variation thin. (L&Z use individual FE because their outcome is the annual number of births of all women, with no risk set.)
- LPM rather than logit: with city FE, logit has an incidental parameters problem and its coefficients cannot be read directly as pp; at an event rate of 7–8% the difference in marginal effects is negligible.

#### c. DDD

$$y_{it} = \beta_1 (T Post) + \beta_2 (T\,Post\,son) + \beta_3 (Post\,son) + \beta_4 (T\,son) + \beta_5 son + \Gamma' X_{it} + \mu_{c(i)} + \lambda_t + \varepsilon_{it}$$

β₄ (T×son) must be included: in the pre-period the daughter group has a real level premium in treated areas (−2.14pp, p=0.006) — precisely the imprint of "stronger son-seeking motive in treated areas" (echoing Part 2); omitting it would push the pre-period gap into the triple interaction and attenuate β₂. β₁ = daughter-group effect, β₁+β₂ = son-group effect.

#### d. Event study

$$y_{it} = \sum_{k \ne 2015} \beta_k\, T_i \times \mathbb{1}\{t=k\} + \Gamma' X_{it} + \mu_{c(i)} + \lambda_t + \varepsilon_{it}$$

β₂₀₁₀…β₂₀₁₄ = year-by-year pre-trend tests (all flat for the daughter group — the core upgrade of the annual design over the wave level: at the wave level the test was possible only against the pooled 2012+2014 baseline).

#### e. Reasons for replacing the wave level with the annual hazard
1. Measurement: wave-level `new_birth` uses the contemporaneous roster and systematically underreports by 20–35% (worse in early waves); the annual design reconstructs from the latest roster.
2. Testability of pre-trends: year by year vs only two pre waves.
3. Power: daughter arm 17,000 woman-years vs ~100–125 obs per wave per arm at the wave level.
4. Time resolution: the boom → fall-back → suppression structure can be distinguished; the wave level blurs it.
The wave level is demoted to robustness (the four-layer arc also holds at the wave level, serving as cross-validation); the layer-4 education spending and the intention analyses stay at the wave level.

#### f. Layer 4 specification (wave level)

$$\text{ihs}(eduexp_{iw}) = \beta^{edu}(T_i \times Post_w) + \Gamma' X_{iw} + \mu_{c(i)} + \lambda_w + \varepsilon_{iw}$$

eduexp: 2012→`fp508`, 2014+→`fp510` (wave-specific; `fp510` in 2012 is fitness spending, already handled); ihs = log(x+√(x²+1)); the extensive margin is also reported. Optional formalisation: a stacked SUR jointly testing β^birth<0 ∧ β^edu>0, or a Wald ratio for magnitude conversion (stating that it is not an IV causal chain).

#### g. Identifying assumptions and inference
- Parallel trends: tested directly by the annual event study; clean for the daughter group, and the coefficient increases rather than decreases after adding group trends.
- No interfering confounders: the universal two-child policy is nationwide and simultaneous ⇒ λ_t absorbs the common component; its interaction with the treatment is precisely the object of study. The son group (the margin newly opened by the policy) is flat at zero throughout = the two-child policy itself did not raise fertility differentially in treated areas.
- Stable composition: the treatment is anchored to the baseline residence.
- Inference: city clustering (~113); wild cluster bootstrap for thin cells (done for the traditional-culture part, still to be added for the main headline — Part 4, question 5); dropping one province at a time; robustness to distance cutoffs.

### 1.5 Two independent explanations of the ≈0 average effect

**(1) Affordability gradient — "who can afford it"** (`tenure_wealth_cost.py`):
- The binary owner/renter split has no power (91.6% are single-home owners) — the variation is within owners.
- Horse-race on the second-birth margin within owners: T×post **−1.57pp (p=0.079)** + T×post×house value z **+1.26pp (p=0.038)** + T×post×income z **+1.68pp (p=0.003)**; the two have a correlation of only 0.17 and each provides insurance independently.
- Reading: the suppression concentrates among constrained owners with low property value / low income; wealthy families are unaffected or even benefit — the net effect goes to zero.
- The debt burden (mortgage) and grandparent coresidence channels are both null: what matters is the level of wealth/income, not debt or the supply of care.

**(2) Cultural gradient — "who wants it"** (Part 2): low-traditional-norm cities have no son-seeking margin to suppress. The top-tercile cities carry the entire effect, and the bottom is exactly zero.

The two gradients are complementary (the horse-race shows they are independent), and both point to: the average masks a highly structured response — this is itself a contribution to the "house prices–fertility" literature (estimates of the average effect in that literature diverge widely, and here we show why).

### 1.6 Supporting evidence

**a. Replication of L&Z Table 3** (`replicate_liu_zhang.py`): reconstructing their annual birth panel (women 15–44 including unmarried, individual+year FE), group-trend specification −0.014 (p=0.052) vs their −0.026; extended to 2022 −0.016 (p=0.035); no-trend specification ≈0. The magnitude gap has been diagnosed: not geography (robust under all distance definitions), but age composition (effect concentrated at ages 15–29) + the roster window (they stop at the 2020 wave ⇒ post-period births are necessarily underestimated).
**b. Couple decision-weight mechanism layer** (2.7): the shock produces a net transfer of fertility decision weight from the husband to the wife, and in daughter one-child families husbands want sons significantly more — intra-couple bargaining explains why it is the son-seeking second birth that is suppressed.

## Part 2: Traditional-culture moderator

Two directions were proposed in the 6/25 discussion: (1) the strength of traditional culture / continuing the family line as a moderator (proxy: qm1004); (2) the division of housework/care and the husband's wishes. Direction (1) has been built (`gender_norm_index.py` + `gender_norm_robustness.py`) and is reported in this part; the status of direction (2) is in 2.7.

### 2.1 Index construction

**Data**: the 2014 attitude battery of all adult respondents (not limited to the women in the analysis sample). This design sidesteps the N wall of intention variables — the moderator is a fixed city-level characteristic (estimated from all adults), and the outcome remains the full-roster annual hazard, with no loss of fertility sample.

**Two indices**, aggregated to prefecture level via the crosswalk (124 cities, ≥25 respondents, median 210 per city):

1. **`son_norm`** = city mean of qm1004: "To continue the family line, a woman should have at least one son" (1–5 agreement). Mean 3.40, sd 0.51, range across cities [2.09, 4.66].
2. **`trad_pc1`** = PC1 of the 10-question traditional-attitudes battery (33% of variance explained, city-level correlation with son_norm 0.84):

| Item | Content | PC1 loading |
|---|---|---|
| qm1001 | Regardless of how parents treat them, children should treat their parents well | +0.17 |
| qm1002 | Children should fulfil their parents' wishes rather than their own | +0.34 |
| qm1003 | A man should live with his parents after marriage | +0.33 |
| **qm1004** | **A woman should have at least one son to continue the family line** | **+0.38 (largest)** |
| qm1005 | One should strive for achievements that bring honour to one's ancestors | +0.36 |
| qm1006 | Children working away from home should still visit their parents often | +0.21 |
| qm1101 | Men should focus on work outside, women on the home | +0.38 |
| qm1102 | For a woman, marrying well matters more than doing well in work | +0.35 |
| qm1103 | A woman should have at least one child | +0.34 |
| qm1104 (reversed) | Men should do half of the housework | −0.22 |

**Usage**: fixed city moderator × annual daughter-group second-birth hazard (the 1.4b equation plus T×post×norm and all lower-order interactions; city FE absorb the level terms of norm and T). Three cuts: continuous interaction, tercile gradient, high/low half samples.

**Who the high-norm cities are (face validity)**: the top tercile (37 cities, list in `output/tables/high_norm_cities.txt`) picks out precisely the strongholds of lineage culture — on the treated side, the eastern and southern Henan plain (Kaifeng 4.66, highest in the whole sample, Shangqiu, Zhoukou, Zhumadian, Nanyang; the Zhengzhou restriction spillover ring) and western Guangdong–Chaoshan (Zhanjiang 4.54, Jieyang, Maoming, Yunfu; the Pearl River Delta spillover ring); on the control side, central Gansu (Linxia, Qingyang, Dingxi, Longnan), western Liaoning (Chaoyang, Liaoyang) and the southwestern ethnic-minority areas (Liangshan, Garzê, Qiandongnan). The index hits the ancestral-hall / genealogy culture belt, not a random set of poor cities.

### 2.2 Results

**(a) Validation of the index (before any DID)** — the pre-2018 "son-seeking premium" (daughter-group − son-group second-birth hazard) rises monotonically with norm tercile:

| Tercile | Daughter-group hazard | Son-group hazard | Son-seeking premium |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low | 3.4% | 1.8% | +1.5pp |
| Middle | 7.9% | 4.8% | +3.1pp |
| High | 17.0% | 10.8% | **+6.2pp** |

⇒ The index measures the son-seeking motive itself, not an abstract "traditionalism".

**(b) Core finding — the suppression is concentrated in traditional cities** (daughter group T×post, split by tercile):

| Tercile | son_norm | trad_pc1 |
|---|---|---|
| T1 low | −0.05pp / −2% (p=0.97, N=7,163) | +0.5pp / +11% (p=0.71, N=6,334) |
| T2 middle | −0.7pp / −6% (p=0.77, N=5,104) | −1.8pp / −17% (p=0.28, N=5,568) |
| T3 high | **−4.9pp / −28% (p=0.026, N=4,423)** | **−5.6pp / −41% (p=0.018, N=4,788)** |

(% = share of that tercile's pre-period hazard.) High/low half samples: trad_pc1 high half −3.5pp (p=0.046), low half −0.7pp (p=0.55). The entire headline effect is carried by high-norm cities; low-norm cities show no movement throughout.

**(c) Horse-race — the norm is not a proxy for urbanisation/income**:

| Triple interactions in the same equation | Coefficient | p |
|---|---|---|
| T×post×son_normz | **−2.58pp** | **0.071** |
| T×post×urbanisation rate z | −0.00pp | 0.997 |
| T×post×city income z | −1.44pp | 0.373 |

son_norm has a correlation of −0.47 with city income — the insurance effect of income (1.5) was previously masking the cultural gradient: with the norm alone the continuous interaction has p=0.17, and after controlling for income p=0.071. The urbanisation-rate interaction is exactly zero.

**(d) Event study (trad_pc1 high/low half samples, daughter arm)**: the high-norm half is flat throughout the pre-period (2010–14) (largest |coefficient| 3.0pp, p≥0.37), and in the post period deepens year by year from 2018: −1.9 → −3.4 → −5.1 → **−7.3pp (2021, p=0.057)**; the low-norm half shows no negative movement at all in the post period (weakly positive in 2017/2019). This decomposition also serves the layer-3 timeline (1.3 layer 3, "How to read the timeline").

**(e) Placebo and boundaries**:
- Son-group continuous interaction −0.83pp (p=0.29), clean.
- Triple interaction in the gender-pooled one-child risk set −1.27pp (p=0.075) — the norm also grades the pooled layer-2 effect, consistent with "the pooled estimate is diluted by the son group".
- 4-way DDD (norm grading of the gender difference) has the right direction but is ns (+0.99pp, p=0.45) — this layer is not claimed.
- gap_mf (norm difference between male and female respondents) null — use the overall norm, without distinguishing "whose norm".

**(f) Competing explanations — H1 wins**:
- H1 "norm = motive": only traditional cities have a son-seeking margin to suppress → predicts larger suppression where norms are high. ✅
- H2 "norm = commitment": traditional families will have a son regardless of house prices → predicts smaller suppression where norms are high. ❌
- Reading: culture provides the motive, house prices determine whether the motive can be carried out — norms tell us "who is at the margin", and the price shock tells us "whose margin is crushed".

### 2.3 Robustness (`gender_norm_robustness.py`)

**(a) Wild cluster bootstrap** (Rademacher, null imposed, 4999 reps, top tercile G=37):

| Index | Estimate | t | WCB p |
|---|---|---|---|
| son_norm T3 | −4.9pp | −2.24 | **0.041** |
| trad_pc1 T3 | −5.6pp | −2.39 | **0.028** |

**(b) Dropping one province at a time (top tercile) — not driven by a single province**:
- Dropping Henan (the largest treated province, 1,081 woman-years) actually strengthens it: −6.4pp (p=0.006); dropping Guangdong −4.2pp (p=0.093).
- The weakest drops are control provinces: dropping Gansu (the largest control province) → −3.5pp (p=0.142) — what is removed is counterfactual mass, not the treatment effect.
- In the son_norm version all 14 drops keep the sign and 12/14 have p<0.10; in the trad_pc1 version all 18 drops have p<0.07.

**(c) Compositional fact**: within the top tercile, treated = the central-eastern lineage belt (8 cities in Henan and 7 in Guangdong, all treated), control = the west/northeast (Gansu, Liaoning, Jilin, all control) — provinces belong almost entirely to T or C. This is an inherent property of the distance-based spillover design (the restricted cities are in the central-east) and is shared by the whole main line; dropping one province at a time + WCB are direct stress tests of it.

### 2.4 Implications for the main line

1. A second independent explanation of the ≈0 average effect: low-norm cities have no son-seeking margin to suppress (complementary to affordability — one is "who wants it", the other "who can afford it").
2. It strengthens the mechanism reading of layer 3: the pre-period T×son premium (−2.1pp, p=0.006) + treated areas being more traditional (next item) + the monotone premium in validation table (a) — together show that "trying for a son" is a real motive.
3. Compositional note: treated cities are themselves more traditional (son_norm 3.59 vs 3.30) ⇒ the headline −2.4pp partly reflects the norm composition of the treated corridor. This does not harm internal validity (identification comes from the pre/post contrast within city FE), but it affects extrapolation: the effect for the national average city would be smaller than the estimate for the treated corridor. The paper states this honestly.
4. Literature hooks: the mirror image of Wei & Zhang (competitive saving: son-driven housing demand) — what house prices suppress is precisely the "trying for a son" margin; the 1.5-child policy / replacement fertility literature; culture × policy-response gradients.

### 2.5 Boundaries of the claim

1. The effect is concentrated in the top tercile; it is a nonlinear gradient: the continuous triple interaction has raw p=0.17 (0.071 after the horse-race), and only the tercile/half-sample cuts are significant — state it as "concentrated in the most traditional third of cities", not "strengthening continuously with norms".
2. 4-way DDD ns — claim only "the daughter-group suppression is graded by norm", not "the norm grades the gender difference".
3. The norm is a reflexive/endogenous city characteristic ⇒ framed as heterogeneity ("who responds"), not a causal effect of culture.
4. Single-point measurement in 2014 (qm1004 in 2014/2022 is cleanly comparable — re-measurement with 2022 is a candidate next step).
5. Whole-province T/C distribution (2.3c) — reported honestly + stress tests done.

### 2.6 Status of direction (2): couple decision weight

The causal result is solid; the channel is not pinned down:

- **Causal headline** (2014-cohort DID-in-weight): the shock produces a net transfer of fertility decision weight from the husband to the wife — T×post×(wife−husband difference in ideal number of children) = +0.027 (p=0.055; p=0.025 clustering by couple); the pre-period placebo is clean. In control areas realised fertility follows almost only the husband's ideal (∂Y/∂ideal_h=+0.051, wife≈0); in high-pressure areas the wife's ideal becomes equally decisive.
- **Integration with the main line**: (i) in daughter one-child families the number of sons husbands want is significantly larger than wives' (paired t, +0.188, p=0.003; 27% of husbands want more sons vs 7% in the son group), and this gap is specific to daughter one-child families (interaction p=0.031); (ii) the shock specifically suppresses births among couples in which "the wife wants fewer" (T×post×wife_fewer = −0.048, p=0.033, full cohort N=18,024). ⇒ The shock hands decision power to the wife → on the son dimension the wife is precisely the party that "wants fewer" in daughter families → the son-seeking second birth is suppressed.
- **Channel not confirmed**: the income/bargaining channel is falsified (null first stage: the shock does not raise the wife's income share or employment); the 2014 housework-hours (qq9010) proxy does not confirm the caregiving channel (triple interaction p=0.96). The theoretical framework uses a GHM-style "consent right + non-contractible caregiving input ⇒ residual control right" (`theory_memo_decision_weight.md`), with the channel as an open question.
- **Not yet done**: norm × decision weight (Doepke–Kindermann test) — whether in traditional-norm cities the shock shifts less weight to the wife. The natural next step connecting Part 2 with this line.

## Part 3: A new data-first lead — the "relocation" of sex selection (exploratory, 2026-07-08)

The workflow is **data structure (figures) → variables → model design**: first look purely descriptively at the landscape of the sex composition of births (without running any regression), pass the measurement gate, and only once a story outline holds move to model design. It is currently at the stage where step two is complete and the formal regressions have not been run.

### 3.1 Data structure (figure: `output/figures/sex_landscape.png`)

All famconf roster births (2000–2022), four descriptive facts:

- **Panel A — the baseline signature of sex selection**: the male share of second births split by firstborn sex — in daughter-first families it falls steadily from ~0.63 in the 2000s to ~0.52 after 2018; son-first families have long been near or below the natural level (0.512), and after 2018 actually climb to ~0.55 (the two lines cross, itself a pattern nobody has written about). The classic parity gradient: first births ~0.51, second births 0.55, third births 0.56–0.61 — selection is concentrated at second births and above.
- **Panel B — what the shock suppresses is the selected second birth**: male share of daughter-first second births, T vs C. In the pre-period the two groups almost coincide (2008–15: 0.588 vs 0.588); in 2018–22 they diverge: control 0.529 vs **treated 0.467 (below natural, i.e. mostly girls)**. This corroborates the hazard decomposition in `sex_selection.py` (73% of the suppression comes from son births).
- **Panel C — the mirror-image change at first births (new fact)**: male share of first births T vs C, balanced in the pre-period (0.510 vs 0.503); in 2020–22 **treated 0.620 vs control 0.545** (raw DID ≈ +6.8pp).
- **Panel D — the norm gradient of the first-birth change**: in high-norm cities the male share of first births goes 0.504 → **0.635** (+13pp), in low-norm cities +4pp — an increase three times that of low-norm cities.

**Story outline**: the shock eliminates sex-selected son-seeking second births (B); but in the same period, in the same treated/high-norm cities, sex selection at **first births** rises (C/D) — son preference has not disappeared, but has **migrated** from the "quantity margin" (have a daughter, then try again) to the "selection margin" (expecting to have only one, select at the first birth). If this holds, the policy reading is reversed: the house-price shock did not reduce the missing girls; it moved the missing girls from the second birth to the first birth. The theoretical fulcrum already exists (when expected fertility collapses towards 1, selection moves forward to lower parities — Ebenstein's logic); "housing costs triggering this migration" has no literature.

### 3.2 Measurement gate (passed)

The surge in the male share of births across the whole sample in 2020–22 (0.545–0.570) was first checked for measurement problems (`roster_lag_sex.py`):

- The sex composition of the same birth cohort across wave snapshots is stable (2016 cohort 0.520→0.534; the 2020 cohort count is filled in from 461 to 879 — count underreporting confirmed — but the male share actually rises from 0.544 to 0.564);
- Bucketing by reporting lag is completely flat (0.527/0.525/0.524/0.525) — there is no "girls registered late" gradient;
- Children appearing late (3+ years) are only slightly female-leaning (0.515 vs 0.531) — completion in the 2024 wave would bring the 2022 cohort down by at most ~1pp.
- Composition decomposition: a counterfactual using 2008–15 parity weights = 0.562 vs actual 0.560 — parity composition explains ≈0; the surge is **within parity, concentrated at first births** (0.505 → 0.509 → 0.568).

⇒ Not a roster-timing artifact; it is a real in-sample pattern.

### 3.3 Variables and model design (next step, not run)

- **Outcome**: birth-level 1{male} (the full roster universe, not restricted by the risk set; the sample is larger than in the hazard design).
- **Design 1 (formalising Panel B)**: DID on P(male) for daughter-first second births — city+year FE, mother's age controls, clustered by city, event study; replaces the old underpowered conditional sex-ratio test (p=0.525).
- **Design 2 (formalising Panel C, new)**: P(male) of first births, same specification; + DDD with norm.
- **Three gates that must be passed**: (i) thin cells — ~165 first births per arm in 2020–22, raw gap ~1.4σ, currently a direction, not an inference; (ii) maternal composition — those having first births in 2020–22 are young women who married into the panel later, and the comparability of these mothers on the T/C sides needs checking (age at first birth, year of marriage, urban/rural); (iii) external check — whether the national sex ratio at birth by parity worsened similarly after 2020; if it rose nationally, the contribution is the difference "treated rose more", not the level.

## Part 5: Main challenges and responses

**Q1 Layer 2 pooled is only p=0.10 — does the second-birth margin hold up?**
58% of the pooled one-child risk set are son families — layer 3 shows that the son group has zero response, so the pooled coefficient is diluted (−2.38pp × 42% ≈ −1.0pp, consistent with what is observed). The role of layer 2 is to locate the margin (second birth vs first birth vs third+), and the significance is carried by layer 3 (p=0.024, DDD p=0.019). Third+ being exactly zero is the most informative number in the pooled table.

**Q2 Why is Post=2016 not significant?**
There is structure: the 2016–17 boom (+2.5/+3.1pp) and the post-2018 suppression average out. Post=2018 is the L&Z convention (restrictions took effect at end-2016 + pregnancy lag ≥9 months); the event study presents both stages of the dynamics in full, and tempo-vs-quantum is discussed separately in the paper (the data end in 2022, so quantum can only be bounded).

**Q3 The norm index is endogenous — traditional cities differ in other dimensions too**
Agreed, which is why the framing is heterogeneity rather than a causal effect of culture. Three lines of defence: (i) the validation step first shows that the index measures the son-seeking motive (premium monotone 1.5→3.1→6.2pp); (ii) the horse-race rules out the two most obvious confounding dimensions (the urbanisation rate is exactly zero, and the norm actually strengthens after controlling for income); (iii) the pre-trends of the high-norm half sample are all flat — if "traditional" were merely a proxy for other trending differences, it would show up in the pre-period.

**Q4 Treated areas are more traditional (3.59 vs 3.30) — could the headline just be the cultural difference itself?**
Identification comes from the pre/post contrast within city FE, and the difference in cultural levels is absorbed by the FE; the only threat would be "traditional cities happen to have a fertility turning point after 2018 that is independent of house prices" — but traditional cities in the control group (Gansu/Liaoning/Jilin) do not show the same turning point (this is exactly the meaning of "only dropping control provinces weakens it" in the province-drop exercise). Composition affects extrapolation (the effect for the treated corridor > the national average), and this is stated honestly.

**Q5 With so many cuts, is this a specification search?**
The direction and the proxy (qm1004) were fixed on 6/25, before any estimation; the order of analysis is validation → main interaction → placebo → robustness; all failed directions (grandparents, mortgage, housework channel, 4-way DDD) are recorded, and we do not report only the good ones.

**Q6 Why not use new DiD estimators such as Callaway–Sant'Anna?**
A single treatment date, no staggered adoption, so the TWFE negative-weights problem does not apply; heterogeneity is modelled directly (split samples by gender/norm/affordability). We also ran a doubleml DR-DiD cross-check: −5.5pp (p=0.09) within the common-support sample, clean son placebo, robust sign; but 68% of observations lack propensity overlap (a mechanical result of city-level treatment), so DML in this design is not more informative than FE-DiD — included as a robustness note.

**Q7 The 2018 and 2019 coefficients are ≈0 — the effect appears only in 2020; is it COVID?**
Acknowledged: the split-window DID shows that the suppression is concentrated in 2020–22 (−3.2pp, p=0.013; −3.75pp, p=0.003 with the boom put into the baseline), and 2018–19 is ≈0 relative to the baseline. Three layers of response:
(i) single-year coefficients lacking power is a property of the design (SE ~2pp), and split windows are the right granularity for testing; Post=2018 is an ex-ante window, and the effect appearing in the later part of the window does not change its meaning, nor is the cut point therefore moved to 2020 (that would be a specification search, and the effect period would then fully overlap with the pandemic period).
(ii) The economic timeline is self-consistent: raw premium pre +3.9 → boom +6.0 → 18–19 +3.1 (back to the pre level = aftermath of the boom) → 20–22 +0.5 (erased). The two-child policy brought son-seeking demand forward; those who wanted to have a child rushed to in 2016–17 and exited the risk set; house prices bit on pregnancy decisions from 2019, and with the birth lag ⇒ visible from 2020.
(iii) Confounding by COVID is blocked by three pieces of evidence: year FE absorb nationwide common shocks; the son group experienced the same pandemic but shows only −1.0pp (p=0.29) in 2020–22; the norm gradient shows that the suppression appears only in high-norm cities, and high-norm cities began to decline already in 2018 (before the pandemic) — a COVID story would need "two years early, and hitting only the firstborn-daughter families of high-norm cities in treated areas".
The single-year pull-back in 2022 (−2.0pp, p=0.33) also has a floor effect: the hazards of both groups collapse to 2–3%, compressing the gap that can be shown, and the 2022 cells are the thinnest (322/362 woman-years).
Honest residual: we cannot separate, within 2020–22, the relative shares of "house-price suppression" and "the combined suppression of house prices + COVID on the same marginal families" — but the son/norm placebos show that COVID itself does not produce this pattern.

**Q8 Why no mediation analysis for the education spending layer?**
`new_birth` is a post-treatment variable; putting it on the right-hand side is a bad control. We use parallel reduced forms + the four-piece binding (1.3 layer 4); if formal joint inference is needed, the stacked SUR approach is ready.

**Q9 Are the sample size / number of clusters too small?**
CFPS is not small (one-child risk set 38,614 woman-years); ~113 prefecture clusters is normal-to-high in the DiD literature. What is genuinely thin are specific cells (top tercile G=37) — these have been handled with WCB and pass. The overall sample size is not a weakness; small-sample inference is applied only to locally thin cells.
