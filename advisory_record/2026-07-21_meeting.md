## First, the whole story in sequence

In 2016 the purchase restrictions pushed housing demand into surrounding unrestricted cities and drove up house prices there — this is the shock. On average fertility barely responds (−0.6pp, p=0.13), but this "≈0" is the most informative number in the paper, because every subsequent layer explains why it is zero.

The previous months answered "who responds": only the second-birth margin moves (one-child families −1.4pp; childless and 2+ are both clean zeros), and it is entirely concentrated in families whose firstborn is a daughter (−2.4pp, p=0.024) — son families lie flat at zero year after year, a built-in placebo. Cutting further, the suppression in daughter families is in turn concentrated in traditional-norm cities (top tercile −28%); low-norm cities have no "trying for a son" margin to suppress in the first place. The couple data explain the internal structure of this margin: trying for a son is mainly the husband's preference (in daughter-first one-child families the number of sons husbands want is significantly larger than what wives want); the shock redistributes fertility decision power from the husband to the wife, and the births suppressed are precisely in families where the wife wants fewer children — so the collapse of the daughter-first margin is, in essence, the result of intra-couple bargaining being rewritten by housing costs.

This time the inverted U gathers all of the above into a deeper structure: "contested" has a precise location on the propensity dimension — families with a second-birth probability of about 50%, completely undecided. The suppression effect is proportional to the marginal density Pc(1−Pc) (p=0.023, daughter arm WCB 0.015; replicated in two independent designs); the G4 group carries 66% of the lost births, while the already-decided G5 is entirely unharmed. The three earlier explanations of "why the average is zero" — son families have no margin, low-norm cities have no margin, non-marginal families have no elasticity — are thereby unified into one sentence: only the hesitant minority is elastic to price.

The welfare layer adds the final piece: those who escaped the fertility loss did not escape the bill. G5 keeps having births as before, but takes the heaviest confidence loss (−0.26, p=0.036) — the standard surplus logic: marginal buyers exit, inframarginal buyers keep buying and pay with their surplus. The fertility layer and the welfare layer are two projections of the same shock onto different groups: the hesitant gave up the child (and precisely because they were hesitant, giving up cost them little), the committed kept the child and paid with their confidence in the future.

Finally, the completeness certificate locks the whole structure: free searches with 11 methods and 4 classification units find no affected group outside this theoretical structure.

Compressed into one paragraph: the house-price shock did not make families as a whole have fewer children; it precisely flipped those families for whom having a child or not was completely undecided — firstborn a daughter, living in a traditional city, the husband wanting to try for a son and the wife not, and standing exactly at the decision margin. The shock did not change those who wanted children or those who did not; it simply made the decision for the hesitant — in the direction of "forget it"; and the families that refused to let it decide for them paid the bill with their confidence. This paragraph is roughly the first draft of the abstract.

## 1. Heterogeneity search

Our heterogeneity structure (daughter × norm × affordability) was found in a theory-driven way, so a referee will naturally ask: if the data search for themselves, can they find anything else?

In the past two weeks I have gone through essentially every usable method: causal forest, the Generic ML of Chernozhukov et al. (2018) (BLP + GATES + CLAN, inference by the median over 20 city splits), high-dimensional LASSO (150 2014-baseline variables and their T×Post interactions, including variables that had never entered a model before — subjective housing pressure qn6017, relative status, financial assets, non-housing debt; inference by 10 city half-splits, one half selecting variables and the other half estimating), plus a subset scan over rule space and so on — altogether 11 methods and 4 classification units (individuals / strata / rules / cities).

The answer is very clean: after removing the known moderators nothing is left. The Generic-ML BLP median p=0.67, and 0.83 with the 148-variable wide menu; LASSO has zero survivors after the honest split (the flagged qn6017 was selected in only one of ten splits). Conversely, when the search is left completely free, the best rule it finds is "daughter × high house-price-to-income ratio × urban" — again our theoretical composite, and after accounting for search multiplicity p=0.159, not even significant. So the paper can safely say: this heterogeneity structure has withstood free searches by three standard method families, and has been neither overturned nor surpassed.

A small methodological exhibit picked up along the way: in full-sample LASSO followed by post-selection OLS, the sampling-design variable subpopulation10 is "significant" at p=0.001 — a perfect classroom example of how unreliable selection plus inference on the same data is; it can go in a footnote. In addition, every linear moderator scan in these two weeks came up empty; the real reason is in Part 2: the heterogeneity itself is quadratic, and linear specifications are inherently blind to it.

Scripts/tables: `cddf_spine.py`, `hd_lasso_hte.py`, `effect_subset_scan.py` → txt files with the same names in `output/tables/`.

## 2. New

### How the groups are formed

The idea is simple: give every one-child mother a score — "absent this shock, how likely is she to have a second birth" — then cut five groups from low to high score and see on whom the shock falls (`prognostic_gates.py`).

The score uses HistGBM (shallow trees) to predict "second birth this year" on the annual hazard panel, trained only on pre-policy data from 2009–2015, so the model learns "in the pre-shock world, what kind of people had second births". The features are 16 baseline variables: firstborn sex, birth cohort, age at first birth, education, urban/rural, income, house-price-to-income ratio, homeownership, ideal number of children, son preference, housework hours, city norm and so on. To prevent leakage, cross-fitting is done with 5 folds by city — when a woman is scored, her entire city is excluded from the training set — then repeated with 10 random seeds, taking each person's median. Finally the women are cut into five equal parts at the woman level, about 1,399 women per group, with G5 the most likely to give birth.

The advantages: the score is entirely a function of baseline variables and the outcome is not used to select groups, so running DID by these groups is legitimate; each group also separately passes a pre-trend gate (none of the 2010–14 yearly coefficients for G4 and G5 are significant). The score does measure what it is meant to measure: its correlation with the actual 2009–15 hazard is 0.40, and the group baseline hazard rises monotonically from 0.6% to 19.3%. (The OOF R² is only 0.08, but annual birth events at the individual level are inherently at this noise level — what is needed here is ranking ability, not precise year-by-year prediction.)

The five groups look like this (woman-level group means):

| | G1 | G2 | G3 | G4 | G5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Baseline annual hazard | 0.006 | 0.026 | 0.071 | 0.129 | 0.193 |
| Firstborn is a son | 71% | 57% | 52% | 52% | 40% |
| Birth cohort | 1971 | 1980 | 1985 | 1987 | 1988 |
| Age at first birth | 26.0 | 25.4 | 24.6 | 23.7 | 22.5 |
| Years of education | 10.7 | 10.4 | 9.8 | 9.3 | 7.9 |
| Urban share | 82% | 71% | 64% | 53% | 47% |
| ln household income per capita | 9.70 | 9.41 | 9.18 | 8.87 | 8.49 |
| Ideal number of children | 1.45 | 1.68 | 1.80 | 1.88 | 2.13 |
| Individual son preference (1–5) | 2.33 | 2.31 | 2.50 | 2.88 | 3.37 |
| City son-norm | 2.92 | 3.10 | 3.39 | 3.68 | 3.93 |

Intuitively: G1 are older, urban, highly educated families whose firstborn is mostly a son, and whose fertility is basically complete; G5 are young early-childbearing, rural, traditional families, mostly with a daughter first, for whom a second child is nearly certain; G4 is the half-urban, half-rural middle layer — traditional enough to want a second child, yet constrained enough to hesitate, standing exactly at the decision margin. One compositional fact must be stated honestly: the treated share rises across groups (G1 41% → G5 60%), because the treated corridor itself is more traditional, which is the same thing seen in the norm layer; the within-group DID is backed by city FE, but this must be mentioned in the presentation.

### Results

Running DID by these groups produces an inverted U — the suppression is not where propensity is highest, but in the upper middle:

| Propensity group | Baseline annual hazard | DID (Post=2018) | Implied share of birth losses |
|---|---|---|---|
| G1 | 0.006 | +0.3pp (ns) | 0% |
| G2 | 0.026 | −0.5pp (ns) | 12% |
| G3 | 0.071 | −0.4pp (ns) | 10% |
| G4 | 0.129 | **−2.9pp (p=0.092)** | **66%** |
| G5 | 0.193 | −0.8pp (ns) | 14% |

There is an accompanying four-panel figure (`output/figures/inverted_u.png`, script `plot_inverted_u.py`): A is the inverted U itself, B is group validity (baseline hazard monotone), C is the positions of the five groups on the density curve (G4 sits exactly at the peak), D is the concentration of lost births. In the meeting, showing A and B side by side is the most intuitive: motive rises monotonically from G1 to G5, but the effect stops at G4 — high motive does not mean elasticity; only standing at the margin brings elasticity.

Theoretically, this is exactly the prediction of a threshold-crossing model: the effect of a cost shock on birth probability depends on how many families are at the decision margin, not on the propensity level itself. G4's annual hazard of 0.129 corresponds to a five-year cumulative probability of about 0.50, exactly the peak of the density; for G5-type families, with first birth at 22.5, rural, norm 3.9, a second child is a certainty, and this much cost cannot push them across the threshold. This also gives a third explanation of "average effect ≈0": only the minority at the margin is elastic to price (the first two explanations are that son families have no margin and low-norm cities have no margin — the three statements are in fact different projections of the same sentence).

### Two tests

The inverted U faces a challenge that must be addressed first: in a hazard design, high-propensity types are depleted quickly, so what remains of G5 in the post period are the "laggards who have not yet given birth"; the composition changes, which could manufacture an inverted U out of nothing. In addition, "G4 is largest" is a shape seen after the fact, and is better upgraded into a prediction that could be written down beforehand. So two tests were run (`inverted_u_tests.py`).

**Test 1: cohort cumulative DID.** Switch to a design immune to risk-set depletion — one row per woman, asking "was there a second birth within 4 years of the first birth", comparing the 2009–13 first-birth cohorts (window entirely before the policy) with the 2016–18 cohorts:

$$Y_i = \mathbf{1}\{by^2_i - by^1_i \le K\},\quad Y_i = \alpha_{c(i)} + \lambda_{by^1(i)} + \beta\, T_i \times Post^c_i + f(agefb_i) + \varepsilon_i$$

Results (K=4): overall −10.6pp (base 38.1%, p=0.035); daughter-first −18.9pp (p=0.040), son-first −8.2pp (ns) — the four-layer arc reproduces on the cumulative scale. More importantly, the shape also reproduces, and is still non-monotone: G2 −1.9 ns / G3 +2.0 ns / **G4 −24.3pp (p=0.035)** / G5 −11.6 ns. If it were a depletion artifact, we would see a monotone gradient with G5 largest here — this does not appear. K=5 has the same shape; with K=3, G5≈G4, as shortening the window pushes the margin upward, which also fits the model.

**Test 2: density structural test.** Compress the shape into an ex ante prediction about one coefficient. The threshold model says the effect should be proportional to the marginal density $dens_i = P^c_i(1-P^c_i)$ ($P^c_i = 1-(1-\hat p_i)^5$ is the cumulative probability, with the density peak at 0.5), while a linear $P^c$ term should itself be useless:

| Design | T×Post×dens | T×Post×Pc (linear) |
|---|---|---|
| Annual hazard (full sample) | −0.261 (p=0.023; WCB p=0.052) | +0.06 (ns) |
| Cohort cumulative scale | −1.88 (p=0.035) | +0.26 (ns) |
| Daughter arm | **−0.485 (p=0.008; WCB p=0.0148)** | ns |
| Son arm (placebo) | −0.072 (WCB p=0.67) ✓ | ns |
| Linear-only benchmark | — | −0.016 (p=0.64), finds nothing |

Three readings. First, the theoretically correct functional form finds what no linear specification can, which also explains why the linear scans in Part 1 all came up empty. Second, the son placebo also holds inside the structural test: the density effect is carried entirely by the daughter arm, showing that the inverted U and layer 3 are the same object — the "contested margin" is precisely daughter families standing at the propensity margin. Third, the couple data provide side evidence: the cell "wife wants fewer × high propensity" is almost empty (only 23 of 1,047 couples) — certain births are consensual births; the high-propensity end has no price elasticity precisely because there is no room for dispute.

### Points that need care

One corollary honestly failed: the 2016–17 boom is not concentrated at the margin (T×boom×dens ≈0), so we cannot write "marginal families rushed ahead and then retreated"; the boom is still described as a response to the two-child policy. Other points: the construction of Pc is an approximation; corr(Pc, dens)=0.75, but the two are separately identified; the full-sample WCB 0.052 is marginal, and the sharp result is in the daughter arm; G1's baseline hazard of 0.006 is close to degenerate; the post group of the cohort design has only 504 women.

Scripts/tables: `prognostic_gates.py`, `inverted_u_tests.py` → txt files with the same names in `output/tables/`.

## 3. Welfare layer: the people harmed are not the same group

First, the confidence layer (qn12014→qn12016, six waves 2012–2022, one-child status, city+year FE) was rebuilt in a reproducible script, and is somewhat stronger than the earlier in-session version: all one-child −0.122 (p=0.008), wants-2nd families −0.185 (p<0.001), content families −0.070 (p=0.41, the zero anchor holds).

Then I tried an ambitious test: are the fertility loss and the welfare loss the same group of people (confidence ~ T×Post×dens)? The answer is no — the dens term is +0.12 (p=0.86), and in the daughter arm the sign is even reversed. By group, the confidence DID broadly increases with propensity rather than forming an inverted U: G1 ≈0 → G3 −0.18 (p=0.034) → G5 −0.26 (p=0.036).

But this "no" in fact points to a better structure. The key juxtaposition: **G5's fertility is unaffected (DID≈0), yet its confidence loss is the heaviest (−0.26, p=0.036)**. This is exactly the standard surplus logic of a price increase: marginal buyers exit (the quantity response is proportional to the marginal density), inframarginal buyers keep buying and lose surplus (proportional to demand). The fertility layer and the welfare layer are two complementary projections of the same shock, and their different shapes are themselves what theory predicts:

| | Fertility response | Welfare response |
|---|---|---|
| Shape | Density-shaped (largest at the margin) | Increasing with demand (largest in G5) |
| Who bears it | Marginal families: give up the birth | Inframarginal families: give birth anyway, pay with welfare |

In one sentence: the house-price shock removes births at the decision margin and removes welfare in proportion to demand — families that still had a child despite high costs paid for that child with their confidence.

When writing, a few points must be honest: the surplus framework is a post-hoc interpretation after the strong test was rejected, and the essay must label it as such (the hardest piece of evidence is the G5 group with "fertility unharmed + confidence damaged", with formal estimates on both sides); the group pattern of confidence is noisy (G3>G4, not strictly monotone), so only write "the high-propensity end bears the largest welfare loss", not a monotone gradient; a density-shaped confidence dip appeared only briefly in 2018 (p=0.014) and faded by 2020–22 — the persistent loss is demand-shaped, not margin-shaped.

Scripts/tables: `welfare_margin_triangulation.py` → `output/tables/welfare_margin_triangulation.txt`.

## 4. Downgrading of several old leads

1. **The income distribution effect is downgraded to suggestive.** The "traditional-poor income loss" that kept surfacing in various classification scans did not pass the formal continuous-gradient test (triple −0.098, WCB p=0.139); the signal is mainly in the rural component, and only the 2022 endpoint is significant. There is also a hard data flaw: the `fam_income_pc` column is entirely missing for 2010, so the income DID has only one pre-period point. At most, a paragraph of discussion with caveats.
2. **The "relocation" of firstborn sex selection is downgraded to a footnote.** The formal test did not pass (+7.8pp, p≈0.23; the norm gradient disappears in the regression; the event study even flips negative in 2022). Kept as an outlook item to "revisit with the 2024 wave".
3. **City-level realised price-increase dose.** The first stage is real (treated cities rose by an extra 9.7 log points, p=0.011), but the fertility suppression is concentrated in treated cities with low price growth — a dose reversal (diff p=0.031), and it cannot be separated from norm composition (collinear across 95 cities). Use it as material for the mechanism discussion of "why it is not a naive price-level channel", supporting an expectations / anticipated-cost interpretation.

## Why the G3 confidence loss is also significant (−0.18, p=0.034)

This is actually not anomalous; there is a fairly natural explanation, and the key is that **the welfare loss follows "demand", and demand is much flatter than "propensity"**.

Look at the contrast between two rows of the five-group profile table: the baseline hazard goes from 0.006 → 0.193 from G1 to G5, more than a 30-fold increase, very steep; but the ideal number of children goes only 1.45 → 1.68 → 1.80 → 1.88 → 2.13, much flatter. That is, "whether they will actually give birth soon" (hazard/propensity) opens up a huge gap across groups, but "whether they want a second child" (demand, i.e. how much surplus there is to lose) differs little across G2–G5 — G3's ideal number of children of 1.80 means that a considerable share of G3 families want a second child but have a low near-term probability of realising it (perhaps postponing, perhaps waiting to see). By the surplus logic, welfare loss ∝ strength of demand, so G3 with upper-middle demand should have an upper-middle confidence loss; −0.18 sits exactly between G1's ≈0 and G5's −0.26, and the shape is self-consistent. Only G1 (ideal 1.45, the only group clearly "not wanting") lies cleanly at zero.

This also echoes the fact that wants-2nd is the cleanest welfare moderator: the confidence loss tracks "whether there is unmet fertility demand", not "whether they will give birth soon". G3 contains many families that "want it but not yet" — the shock made this future option more expensive, its option value shrinks, and confidence drops in the current period, even though there is little current fertility behaviour to suppress.

What really needs explaining is not that G3 is large but that **G4 is small** (−0.086, ns) — it breaks monotonicity. The honest reading is the one already written in the document: the group pattern of confidence is noisy, the confidence intervals of G3 and G4 overlap heavily (G3 has 2,474 observations, G4 only 1,849, so G4's standard error is larger), and the two groups cannot be statistically distinguished. So the wording stays "the welfare loss tilts towards the high-propensity end", without writing a monotone gradient, and there is no need to tell a separate story about G3 — its being significant is itself consistent with the demand shape.

If one wants to say one more thing (speculative; can be raised in the meeting, should be written cautiously in the paper): G3 is more urban than G4/G5 (64% vs 53%/47%), and confidence as an outcome is an overall assessment of "the future", not only running through the fertility channel — urban families are more directly exposed to rising house prices, and a pure housing-cost/wealth-expectations channel would also depress confidence. This could explain a little more of G3's loss, but cannot be tested separately; it is material for discussion.
