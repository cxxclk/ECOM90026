# Advisor Meeting Summary — 2026-06-11

ECOM90026 · House prices and fertility (HPR spillover DID) · Status, findings and questions for discussion

---

## 0. One-sentence summary

The identification problem has been solved (we obtained a county→city crosswalk; city-level matching covers 85% of the sample). Following the HPR spillover design of Liu & Zhang (2025), we rebuilt the treatment, and the main results are in: **the house-price shock did not lower fertility on average, but it significantly suppressed the son-seeking second births of "one-child families whose firstborn is a daughter", and these families redirected resources into the education of their existing child (quantity→quality substitution)**. The story is locked; what remains is mechanism additions, robustness and writing.

## 1. Progress since the last discussion (April)

At our last meeting the core obstacle was that the CFPS county codes were masked, so city-level matching was impossible, and I was still considering switching to the monetised shantytown resettlement programme as the shock. Both issues are now resolved or no longer necessary:

- **City matching solved**: found the `顺序码匹配.dta` crosswalk (countyid → prefecture-level city, 162 counties → 127 cities, 99.9% validated), covering ~85% of the analysis sample.
- **HPR spillover treatment built**: following the design of the base paper, `treat_hpr = 1` for unrestricted prefecture-level cities ≤250 km from one of the 22 restricted cities (the restricted source cities themselves are dropped), with distances computed from centroids in a China shapefile. Merged into the main data pipeline.
- **Monetised shantytown resettlement downgraded**: it was originally an alternative "forced" by the county-level masking; now that the problem is solved, it is kept only as an optional robustness shock.
- Sample: married women aged 20–45, six waves 2012–2022, 20,528 observations / 10,098 individuals; the standard specification is **city + year FE** (no individual FE — it would absorb the cross-prefecture variation that identifies the spillover effect), clustered by city, with the baseline being the pooled 2012+2014 pre-period.

## 2. Research question and answer

> **Question**: After the 2016 purchase restrictions pushed housing demand (and prices) into surrounding unrestricted cities, which families stopped having children? Where did the money saved go?
>
> **Answer**: The price shock did not lower fertility on average — what it crushed was **the son-seeking second-birth margin of one-child families with a firstborn daughter**, and these families redirected resources into **the education of the existing daughter**.

## 3. Four layers of empirical results (core numbers)

| Layer | Finding | Key numbers |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Average effect ≈ 0** | Pooled DID has the right sign but is not significant; the average masks the true margin | city+year FE −0.8pp (p=0.33); only 2022 significant (−3.3pp, p=0.03) |
| **2. The margin is the second birth** | By pre-shock parity: the effect concentrates in one-child families; childless and 2+ are both zero | one-child −2.3~−2.8pp (p=0.10–0.18); childless +0.4pp ns; 2+ ≈0 ns |
| **3. The margin is gendered** | The suppression comes entirely from **firstborn-daughter** families; firstborn-son families are ≈0 in every year, equivalent to a built-in placebo | daughter group DID **−6.2pp (p=0.031)**, rural-led (−9.9pp); son group +0.7pp (p=0.75); DDD T×post×son **+7.7pp (p=0.031)**; event study deepens year by year: 2016 −3.1 → 2022 **−11.6pp (p<0.001)**; firstborn sex balanced T/C (p=0.86) |
| **4. Q→Q substitution** | The same daughter families **increase education spending**, with a time path mirroring the fertility decline; 2+ families (no quantity margin) show no response | daughter group ihs(edu) DID **+0.89 (p=0.034)**, extensive **+10.5pp (p=0.026)**; ES last two periods +1.27/+1.46 both significant; 2+ benchmark group −0.12 (p=0.64) |

**Why the gender result is the contribution**: ex ante, two channels predicted opposite directions —
- **Marriage-housing burden channel** (sons will need to buy a marriage home → higher prices raise the shadow liability of son families) → predicts that son families contract. **Rejected**.
- **Son preference / replacement channel** (firstborn-daughter families are the active "trying for a son" second-birth margin) → predicts that daughter families contract. **Holds**.

Before 2016, firstborn-daughter families were the *legal* second-birth margin (rural 1.5-child policy: a second child is allowed if the first is a girl). Their second-birth premium in treated areas (19–25% vs ~11.5% in the control group) fell to zero after the shock. By contrast, the margin newly opened for son families by the universal two-child policy is **completely inelastic** to price — this turns the universal two-child policy, a confounder, into the object of study itself: *housing costs determined who responded to the policy*.

## 4. Differentiation from the base paper

Liu & Zhang (2025, `original paper.pdf`): same identification strategy, but their margins are **marriage-market entry and education costs for rural non-owners**. Our margin is **within-marriage parity progression × son preference × Q→Q substitution** — neither the mechanism nor the outcome set overlaps. Literature hooks: Wei & Zhang (competitive saving, son-driven housing demand — we find that the margin that is elastic to price is precisely the *reverse* one), the Becker Q–Q tradeoff (layer 4 is direct expenditure evidence), and the 1.5-child policy literature.

Strategically we lean towards **Strategy A**: honest replication + mechanism as the contribution (~114 prefecture-level city clusters is the CFPS coverage limit, so we cannot aim for more power than the base paper).

### Did the original paper also find an effect only in subgroups? — No, but our average ≈0 is to be expected

In the original paper **the average effect itself is significant**, at two levels: (i) prefecture level (birth rates from statistical bulletins, annual 2009–2021): treated areas fall by 1.68‰ on average, semi-elasticity −8.7, **significant at 1%** — this is their headline; (ii) individual level (full CFPS sample, 80,177 obs): Treat×Post ≈ **−0.025** (mean 0.061), **significant at 5%** in all four specifications (Table 3). The subgroup (rural, no urban housing) is mechanism evidence: after splitting, **only this group is individually significant**, and the other housing-status groups are all ≈0 — they also have the structure of "the average masks heterogeneity", except that their significant group happens to be the majority of the sample, so the average is significant too.

Our insignificant average (p=0.33) **does not contradict** this, for three reasons:
1. **Sample definition**: they use all women of childbearing age (including unmarried), and half of their mechanism runs through **marriage-market entry** (price↑ → marriage↓ → births↓); we restrict to married women, which directly shuts this channel — by their own decomposition, a weaker average for the remaining within-marriage margin is expected.
2. **Data structure**: they rebuild the biennial CFPS into **annual** birth records (2009–2020, 80k obs, annual pre-trends + individual FE + treatment-specific linear trends); we use wave-level new_birth (20.5k obs).
3. **Time window**: their Post starts in 2018 and their sample ends in 2020, so they do not have our thin-cell problem in 2022.

**How to put it in the meeting**: we did not "fail to replicate"; rather we "decomposed what they left at the aggregate level" — after shutting the marriage-market entry margin, the average within-marriage margin is ≈0, and only by dissecting down to parity × firstborn sex did we find the active margin where the real price elasticity lies.

### Direct replication results (2026-06-11, `scripts/replicate_liu_zhang.py`)

Following their Table 3 specification, we rebuilt the **annual** birth panel (famconf child birth years → count of newborns per woman per year, ages 15–44 including unmarried, Post=2018, individual+year FE, clustered by prefecture, restricted source cities dropped). Their DID does not need house-price data (Treat is purely distance-defined), so apart from distances coming from shapefile centroids, the inputs are fully from the same source (CFPS).

| Specification | Ours | Theirs (Table 3) |
|---|---|---|
| No trend adjustment (they have no such column) | −0.004 (p=0.56) | — |
| + prefecture linear trends [col 1] | −0.011 (p=0.16) | **−0.023** (p<0.05) |
| + group linear trends [col 2] | **−0.014 (p=0.052)** | **−0.026** (p<0.05) |
| Same, extended to 2022 | **−0.016 (p=0.035)** | (sample ends 2020) |
| Married 20–45 (our restriction), no trend | **−0.016 (p=0.011)** | — |
| Unmarried / out-of-age-range complement, group trends | +0.003 (p=0.90) | — |

N≈94.7k (theirs 80.2k), mean 0.076 (theirs 0.061), 113 prefecture clusters.

**Three key points**:
1. **Qualitative replication succeeds**: the sign agrees, the magnitude is about 55–60% of theirs, p=0.052 under the group-trend specification, and p=0.035 after extending to 2022. The identification strategy holds under our data construction.
2. **Trend adjustment is load-bearing**: without any linear trend the effect is ≈0 — *all* four columns of their Table 3 include trend adjustment (they themselves acknowledge a pre-existing negative trend in treated areas). That is, their "significant average effect" is **conditional on linearly extrapolating the pre-existing trend**. This matters for assessing the strength of evidence in both papers.
3. **Locating why our wave-level average is ≈0**: it is not an identification failure, but (i) outcome construction (wave-level binary vs annual count), (ii) FE structure (city FE vs individual FE + trends), and (iii) no trend adjustment. The annual reconstruction + their specification recovers about 60% of the effect. Moreover, the married subsample is likewise significantly negative in the annual panel, while the complement is zero — the within-marriage margin really exists, consistent with our parity × sex dissection.

**Diagnosing the source of the magnitude gap (−0.014 vs −0.026)** (`scripts/diagnose_distance.py`):
- **It is not the shapefile/distance method**. Urban-core centroids vs whole-city polygon centroids: median distance difference 12 km, only 6/376 prefectures flip at 250 km, coefficient −0.0135 vs −0.0142. Our lower treated share (0.49 vs 0.61 in their Table 1) is most likely because they measured to the **boundary/urban area** of the restricted city rather than the centroid — switching to centroid→boundary (share 0.56) or boundary→boundary (share 0.66) reproduces their share, but the coefficient stays at −0.012~−0.013; a 300 km cutoff gives −0.017 (p=0.021). **The estimate is robust to every distance definition**, so geographic construction can be ruled out.
- **The real source is age composition + the reconstruction window, not the birth records themselves** (same source data; the reconstruction choices differ in measurable ways):
  (i) **The effect is concentrated among the young**: ages 15–29 −0.019 (hazard base 0.138), ages 30–44 −0.003 (base 0.031) — consistent with their "effect at ages 20–29". The earlier finding that "restricting to the 2010 baseline sample flips the coefficient positive" is an **age artifact**: that cohort is on average 35+ in the post period (fertility hazard ≈0.03), and the young person-years carrying the effect come from later aged-in cohorts — not from differences in the birth records.
  (ii) **Roster registration lag**: the famconf roster of the same wave underreports recent births — wave-level `new_birth` is 20–35% below the reconstruction from the latest roster (2012: 0.047 vs 0.073, converging wave by wave, fully identical by 2022; agreement rate 97–99%). They stop at the 2020 wave ⇒ their 2019–20 (post-period) births are necessarily underreported; we complete them with the 2022 roster. Together with their listwise deletion on control variables (N=80k vs our 95k), this jointly explains the residual magnitude gap.
  (iii) ⚠ **By-product warning**: the pipeline's wave-level `new_birth` systematically underestimates births, more severely in early waves — the annual hazard design (latest roster) is better on measurement, which is another argument for adopting the improved design; wave-level results rely on year FE to absorb this bias (unless the underreporting is correlated with treatment, which needs one sentence in the essay).
- How to state it in the meeting: replicated to about 60% of the magnitude, same sign, fully robust to distance definitions; the residual difference is located in age-composition weights and the roster reconstruction window, and at the mechanism level we agree with them (the effect is among the young) — this does not affect the validity of our own design.

## 4.5 Improved main design borrowing from their specification (2026-06-11, `scripts/annual_firstborn_gender.py`)

Layer 3 (firstborn sex) is upgraded from a wave-level binary outcome to an **annual second-birth risk panel** (discrete-time hazard): a woman enters the risk set in years when she has "exactly one child", and the outcome = whether she has a second birth that year. It absorbs four elements from their design — annual reconstruction, **Post=2018** (pregnancy lag), treatment defined by baseline location (to prevent endogenous migration), and group-trend robustness. The risk-set design also fixes two patches of the old design: the pre-period no longer mixes in first births, and the pooled 2012+2014 baseline is no longer needed (year-by-year pre-trends can be tested directly). N=40,108 person-years (daughter group 17,000 / 3,176 women — in the old design each cell had only ~100 obs).

| Result | Numbers |
|---|---|
| Daughter group DID (Post=2018) | **−2.4pp (p=0.024)**, hazard base 8.4% → a decline of about 28% |
| Son group (placebo) | −0.6pp (p=0.46), still flat at zero |
| DDD T×post×son | +1.8pp (p=0.091); T×son pre-period gap −2.1pp (p=0.006) confirmed again |
| + group linear trends | daughter group **strengthens** to −3.5pp (p=0.018), the trend term itself ≈0 |
| Annual pre-trends (2010–2014, relative to 2015) | all within ±1.5pp, none significant — **clean** |

**Two new findings**:
1. **Key contrast with the original paper**: their average effect *depends* on trend adjustment (≈0 without it); our daughter-group effect **does not** (it is stronger with it, and the pre-trend is flat to begin with) — in terms of strength of evidence this exceeds their average result, and is worth emphasising in the meeting.
2. **The time structure appears**: the treated daughter group has a small second-birth peak in 2016–17 (+2.5/+3.1pp, response to the universal two-child policy), turns negative from 2018, and falls to −3.1/−3.6pp in 2020–21; the second-birth premium in treated areas disappears completely (raw: pre-period ~10–11% vs control ~6.5–7%; in 2020–21 both groups fall to ~5.5%). The Post=2016 specification shows ≈0 because it averages in the 2016–17 boom — this explains the sensitivity to the choice of timing, and raises a new tempo vs quantum question (does the early childbearing in 2016–17 partly explain the later gap?).

## 4.6 Model specifications (detailed)

### A. Notation and sample

- $i$ = individual woman, $c(i)$ = the prefecture-level city of her **baseline** (earliest observed wave), $t$ = year (annual design) or wave (wave-level design).
- $T_i \in \{0,1\}$: the baseline city is an unrestricted prefecture within ≤250 km of the nearest restricted city (all 22 restricted source cities are dropped and enter no sample).
- $Post_t$: annual design $= \mathbb{1}\{t \ge 2018\}$ (restrictions took effect late 2016–early 2017 + pregnancy lag, following Liu & Zhang); wave-level design $= \mathbb{1}\{t \ge 2016\}$ (legacy).
- $son_i \in \{0,1\}$: sex of the firstborn (earliest-born child), from the latest famconf roster (`tb2_a_c*`, 1=male).
- All specifications: standard errors **clustered by prefecture** (~113 clusters).

### B. Main design — annual second-birth hazard DID (`annual_firstborn_gender.py`)

**Risk set** (discrete-time hazard): woman $i$ enters the sample in year $t$ if and only if she has **exactly one child** as of the end of year $t-1$, i.e. $t \in (\text{firstby}_i,\ \min(\text{secondby}_i,\ \text{lastwave}_i,\ 2022)]$, and age $\in [20,45]$. She exits after a second birth. Women whose first birth is twins never enter (parity is never 1).

**Split-sample DID** (daughter group / son group estimated separately):

$$y_{it} = \beta\,(T_i \times Post_t) + \gamma_1 age_{it} + \gamma_2 age_{it}^2 + \gamma_3 dur_{it} + \gamma_4 dur_{it}^2 + \mu_{c(i)} + \lambda_t + \varepsilon_{it}$$

where $y_{it} = \mathbb{1}\{i\ \text{has a second birth in year}\ t\}$, $dur_{it} = t - \text{firstby}_i$ (years since the first birth, the duration dependence of the hazard), $\mu_c$ = city FE, $\lambda_t$ = year FE. The main effect of $T_i$ is absorbed by $\mu_c$ and $Post_t$ by $\lambda_t$. **No individual FE**: the treatment variation is in the cross-section of prefectures, and individual FE would absorb the identifying variation together with a large amount of between information; in a hazard panel, individuals entering and exiting the risk set also make the within variation thin.

**DDD** (full set of interactions, single-equation version):

$$y_{it} = \beta_1 (T_i Post_t) + \beta_2 (T_i Post_t son_i) + \beta_3 (Post_t son_i) + \beta_4 (T_i son_i) + \beta_5 son_i + \Gamma' X_{it} + \mu_{c(i)} + \lambda_t + \varepsilon_{it}$$

⚠ $\beta_4$ ($T\times son$) **must be included**: the daughter group has a real pre-period level premium in treated areas (annual version −2.1pp, p=0.006); omitting it would push the pre-period gap into the triple interaction and attenuate $\beta_2$. $\beta_1$ = daughter-group effect, $\beta_1+\beta_2$ = son-group effect.

**Event study** (split sample, 2015 as the omitted base year):

$$y_{it} = \sum_{k \ne 2015} \beta_k\, T_i \times \mathbb{1}\{t=k\} + \Gamma' X_{it} + \mu_{c(i)} + \lambda_t + \varepsilon_{it}$$

$\beta_{2010}\ldots\beta_{2014}$ = year-by-year pre-trend tests (all flat for the daughter group); the post-period coefficients describe the dynamics (2016–17 boom → negative from 2018).

**Robustness**: + group linear trend $T_i\times(t-2009)$ (the daughter-group coefficient strengthens to −3.5pp and the trend term is ≈0 ⇒ no reliance on trend extrapolation, in contrast with the L&Z average effect).

### C. Wave-level DID (legacy → demoted to robustness; current version of layers 1–4)

$$y_{iw} = \beta\,(T_i \times Post_w) + \Gamma' X_{iw} + \mu_{c(i)} + \lambda_w + \varepsilon_{iw}$$

- Sample: married women 20–45, wave ∈ {2012,…,2022}, $y_{iw}$ = `new_birth` (whether a birth occurred between the previous wave and this wave).
- Layers 2/3 split the sample on **2014 baseline parity / firstborn sex** (`n_kids_base`, `firstborn_son` fixed at baseline to avoid endogenous grouping).
- $X_{iw}$ = age, log(income), urban; the event study uses **pooled 2012+2014** as the base (the treated daughter group has a 24.6% spike in 2014).
- Known measurement problem: `new_birth` uses the contemporaneous roster and underreports by 20–35% (worse in early waves) — year FE absorb its common component; this is one of the main reasons for demoting this design.

### D. Education spending equation (Q→Q, wave level, `eduexp_qq_substitution.py`)

**Same sample split and same right-hand side** as C, only the outcome changes:

$$\text{ihs}(eduexp_{iw}) = \beta^{edu}\,(T_i \times Post_w) + \Gamma' X_{iw} + \mu_{c(i)} + \lambda_w + \varepsilon_{iw}$$

- $eduexp$ = famecon household education/training spending (2012→`fp508`, 2014+→`fp510`; `fp510` in 2012 is fitness spending — another 2012 variable collision); ihs = $\log(x+\sqrt{x^2+1})$; the extensive margin $\mathbb{1}\{eduexp>0\}$ is also reported.
- Household-level outcome ⇒ clean only for one-child families (all spending belongs to the only child).
- **Binding logic (not a joint regression)**: the fertility equation and the education equation are parallel reduced forms — `new_birth` is never put on the right-hand side of the education equation (post-treatment mediator = bad control). The "substitution" is bound together by four pieces: in the daughter group, births↓ and education↑; event-study time paths mirror each other; the son group is a double-zero placebo; **families with 2+ children (no quantity margin) show no education response** (ruling out a pure income/price effect explanation).
- Optional formalisation (if the advisor asks): (i) **stacked SUR** — standardise and stack the two outcomes, $y \sim T\times Post \times \text{outcome-type}$, with city clustering covering cross-equation correlation, jointly testing $\beta^{birth}<0 \wedge \beta^{edu}>0$; (ii) **Wald ratio** $\beta^{edu}/\beta^{birth}$ for magnitude conversion (stating that it is not an IV causal chain).

### E. Fertility intentions equation (analysis C, planned)

A third outcome under the same design: $ideal_{iw}$ (ideal number of children, `qm501` 2014 / `qka202` 2018, 2022). The information is in the **comparison of coefficients** rather than a single equation: $\beta^{ideal} \approx 0$ with $\beta^{birth}<0$ ⇒ the constraint binds (unmet fertility demand); $\beta^{ideal}<0$ moving together ⇒ preference adaptation. Few waves (3 waves) + changes in question wording are known risks.

### F. Replication specification (L&Z Table 3, `replicate_liu_zhang.py`, only for reconciliation)

$$newborns_{it} = \beta\,(T_i \times Post_t) + \alpha_i + \lambda_t + [\text{trend}] + \varepsilon_{it}$$

All women of childbearing age 15–44 (including unmarried), annual 2009–2020, $\alpha_i$ = **individual FE** (their specification), trend = prefecture-specific or treatment-group linear trend (each of their four columns has one of these). We use this specification only in the replication; our own analysis uses the city FE convention of B/C.

### G. Identifying assumptions and inference

- **Parallel trends**: tested directly in the annual design (the pre-period coefficients of the event study in B); clean for the daughter group, and not dependent on linear trend extrapolation.
- **No interfering confounders**: the universal two-child policy (2016.1) is nationwide and simultaneous ⇒ its common component is absorbed by $\lambda_t$; its interaction with the treatment is precisely the object of study (who in treated areas responded to the policy).
- **Stable composition**: the treatment is anchored to the baseline residence, preventing self-selection through migration induced by the price shock.
- **Inference**: city clustering (~113); plan to add wild cluster bootstrap (the daughter group's post-period cells are still thin) + dropping one province at a time.

## 5. Honest limitations (will be written into the paper)

1. **Post-period cells are thin**: the daughter subsample has ~100–125 observations per wave per arm; the 2022 control-group birth rate is exactly 0. The equally thin son group is flat at zero throughout, which is the anchor of credibility.
2. **The 2014 treated daughter group has a 24.6% spike** → all baselines use pooled 2012+2014; the pre-trend is clean only relative to the pooled baseline (2012 ES coefficient −7.1pp, p=0.17).
3. **The gender difference in education spending is not significant** (p=0.28): the claim is "within daughter families: fertility↓, education↑", not that the education response differs by gender.
4. `edu_exp` is at the household level — clean for one-child families (all spending = this one child), not clean for 2+ families.
5. The universal two-child policy coincides with HPR (2016.1) — but, as in §3, it has been turned into part of the framework rather than a mere confounder.

## 7. Questions I would like to discuss with you

1. **Sample thinness vs strength of results**: the daughter group has ~100–125 obs/wave/arm in the post period, yet the DDD and event study are significant at conventional levels and the son placebo is clean. At this level of power, how strongly should the conclusions be written? Is small-sample inference such as wild cluster bootstrap needed?
2. **DDD or split-sample DID as the headline**? The DDD (T×post×son = +7.7pp, p=0.031) is more compact but requires explaining the pre-period gap in T×son (−6.4pp, real and must be controlled); the split sample (daughter −6.2pp significant / son ≈0) is more intuitive. Which should be the main table?
3. **Framing of the universal two-child policy**: should the angle "housing costs determined who responded to the two-child policy" be developed openly as a selling point of the paper, or discussed conservatively only as a confounder?
4. **The boundary of the claim in the Q→Q layer**: education spending rises significantly in the daughter group, but the daughter vs son difference is not significant — is the conservative wording in §5.3 acceptable, or is more evidence needed (e.g. itemised spending)?
5. **Is analysis C (fertility intentions) worth doing**? The intention variables exist only in three waves (2014/2018/2022) with changes in wording; if it turns out null, would it weaken the narrative?
6. **Scope confirmation**: is the above + robustness + writing an appropriate final scope for a course paper? Or should some part be cut / strengthened?
7. **[New] Confirmation of the main design migration**: based on the replication findings (wave-level `new_birth` underreports by 20–35%; the annual hazard design has clean pre-trends and much greater power), I plan to move the fertility results (layers 1–3) to the annual hazard as the headline and demote the wave level to robustness; education spending and intentions stay at the wave level. Do you approve of this arrangement?
