Thanks again for the comments after the presentation. Two things I wanted to close the loop on.

**1. Quick confirmation on the ML in the talk.** As I said on the day, the model only ever saw pre-2016 data; its job was to label women by their baseline chance of a second birth, and the DID then ran inside those groups. Since the inverted-U was spotted after the fact, I checked it in two other ways that don't reuse the same object: a cohort version (second birth within four years of the first, 2009–13 vs 2016–18 cohorts, so no at-risk-pool depletion can manufacture a U), where the U reproduces, and a one-parameter structural test with the effect proportional to the density at the decision margin, which passes in the daughter-first arm with son-first as a clean placebo. I'll add a train/predict/test table to the write-up so it's all explicit.

**2. Pre-train, post-predict — tried it, and it works.** I trained a menu of models on 2009–15 only, predicted every woman-year including the post period, and ran the DID on the prediction errors, with year effects so the control group absorbs the model's time drift. The winner (on pre-period out-of-sample log-loss) is an ensemble of XGBoost and HistGBM, but what the model looks at didn't change: city son-norm is still the top feature, then age, with the SES variables barely registering.

The one thing to know is that a second birth in a given year is a rare event (about 8%), so around 90% of the year-level variance is coin-flip noise no model can remove. That shows up as:

- *Annual:* the −2.4pp daughter-first effect splits into roughly −0.9 from the composition of who is still at risk (significant; the women who wanted more children took the birth in the 2016–17 rush and left the pool) and −1.5 within-type, right sign under every model with clean placebos, but not significant on its own.
- *Cumulative (second birth within four years):* less noise, bigger effect, so the residual DID holds up (−16.7pp vs −17.7pp reference) and the composition term is zero. Post cohorts are small until the 2024 wave, so this goes in the appendix.

So nothing overturned, but it explains why the annual result is weaker than the cohort one, and it makes the ML part much easier to present. Happy to go through the tables whenever suits.
